The 2026 midterms, explained without the spin

By The Trends Incoming team · Published 2026-10-14

Nonpartisan explainer: sourced facts, both sides represented, no endorsements.

Election Day is Tuesday, November 3, 2026. Here's the quick version: all 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats and 36 governorships are up. This page is strictly nonpartisan. We report what forecasters and pollsters are publishing, show where they disagree, and don't tell you who to vote for.

What it is

Midterms happen halfway through a presidential term. They decide which party controls the House and the Senate for the next two years, and that shapes everything from budgets to confirmations. This cycle also includes special Senate elections, including races in Ohio and Florida, to fill out existing terms.

Why it's trending now

We're inside three weeks, and a wave of new polls just dropped. One summary of Cook Political Report ratings counts seven Senate races as Toss Ups. Forecast models are publishing daily updates, and campaigns are moving ad money around in public view. When money moves, people notice. NBC reported that the Senate Leadership Fund paused North Carolina ads after spending over $30 million there and booked airtime in Kansas.

What the numbers say (and why you should hold them loosely)

One independent statistical model, Call the Map, put Democrats' chance of winning the House at 81 in 100 and the Senate at 55 in 100 as of October 13. Republicans currently hold 53 Senate seats, so Democrats would need a net gain of four for a majority. These are model probabilities, not predictions, and the model itself says so.

Individual races show how close things are, and how much polls can disagree. Take Ohio's Senate special election, Sherrod Brown (D) vs. Jon Husted (R). A New York Times/Siena poll (Sept 22 to Oct 1) had Brown up 49 to 46. A CNN/SSRS poll (Sept 29 to Oct 5) had Brown up 49 to 43. A co/efficient poll (Oct 5 to 6), from a Republican-aligned firm, had Husted up 46 to 45. Same race, same couple of weeks, different results. That's normal. Different samples and methods produce different numbers, and many leads sit inside the margin of error.

The October 9 ratings roundup listed other close Senate contests, including Alaska, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Michigan, Minnesota and Texas. Polling averages there were mostly within about three points.

The backstory

Historically, the president's party often loses seats in midterms, but "often" isn't "always," and every cycle has its own story. Both parties are arguing their case on the economy, immigration, health care and government spending, and voters will weigh those differently. We'll leave the arguments to the campaigns and link you to the sources.

What's next

  • Now through Nov 3: more polls, debates and rating changes. Expect forecasters to shift as new data lands.
  • Early and mail voting: rules and deadlines vary by state. Check your state election office or vote.gov for your dates.
  • Nov 3: Election Day. Some races, especially close ones and states with ranked-choice voting like Maine, may take days to call.
  • After: a lame-duck Congress returns with a must-pass to-do list, including government funding past Dec 11.

Where to follow it

For forecasts, compare more than one model. For polls, look at averages rather than single surveys, and check who paid for a poll. For results, use your state's official election site and established wire services. The hashtag links below will show you the conversation, which is loud on every side. Our advice: read the source, not just the screenshot.
